Example from CEEZER

Roadmap Balancing

Core Principles

  • Wherever possible, kill two birds with one stone
  • Wherever possible, think forward to the future to avoid creating complexity through technical debt — temporary solutions should be sanitised for clean replacement later on
  • Always evaluate what needs to happen now, and what can wait until later

Context

Core administrative tooling was built years prior around a now-outdated process. As a consequence, the core business process (portfolio creation) was regularly performed offline in spreadsheets, then duplicated manually into the system, before being sold through the platform — a process itself supplemented by yet more spreadsheets.

The Tension

Several competing demands:

  1. A record-breaking RFP from Lufthansa would see millions of dollars' worth of carbon credits distributed across hundreds of subsidiaries. Meeting this would require either thousands of manual actions a month, or a fundamental workflow change.
  2. A business-led executive strategy change, moving the company's primary revenue source away from one-time transactions and towards a more subscription-based "long-term off-take" (LTO) model.
  3. A scalability crisis: not only does duplication of data waste time, it also introduces manual error and sows doubt and confusion in the sales process.
  4. A range of QOL complaints from internal operators.
  5. Engineering wishes to build a stronger internal workflow engine to erase technical debt.

The Judgement Call

The first three of these problems touch on administrative tooling, and each can be split further into separate components.

The Lufthansa revenue is too great to ignore, but the lifecycle management tooling can be split out: a workaround should be put in place to allow the upfront transaction, while the software required to manage each individual credit tranche isn't needed on day one. The first guaranteed operation is renewal after a year, and the unlikely individual interactions before then can be managed internally by operations.

(continued on slide 5)

The Judgement Call (Continued)

The LTO changes can be split the same way — the day-one requirement is that they be configured and sold; portfolio management, annual deliveries, and lifecycle management can follow later.

Scalability: the main focus of these changes is in the configuration and sale of portfolios — the same area the LTO changes require rework in.

QOL fixes and the workflow engine don't block revenue or scalability, so they can be sequenced around the higher-stakes work rather than driving it.

Sequencing

  1. First, prioritise a workaround/MVP for Lufthansa, focused on facilitating the sale, while delaying the lifetime management tooling.
  2. Second, prioritise scalability and align the core product with the process. Within this redesign, extend the scope to include the configuration and sale of LTOs without the need for spreadsheets.
  3. Third, group and prioritise "low-hanging fruit" quality-of-life improvements and automations, focusing on neglected areas.
  4. Fourth, in the second half of the year, build lifetime management for LTOs and Lufthansa — laying the foundation for future workflow engine work along the way.

Outcome

Lufthansa go-live happens smoothly, securing a healthy injection of revenue for the company. The portfolio is represented partially in the system, with the remainder tracked as a deliberate, contained interim workaround by the Finance team.

Core product changes are implemented, aligning process and product, removing spreadsheets from sales calls and reducing data duplication.

Lifecycle management tooling remains on the roadmap for H2, tracking ahead of the one-year renewal deadline.

Demonstrates Prioritisation under competing stakeholder demands, and identifying shared dependencies to sequence work efficiently.
Example from Alteos

Saying No

Core Principles

  • Don't just understand the request, understand the problem
  • Don't just say yes, evaluate the full context before deciding
  • Don't just say no, explain why, and compromise where appropriate

Situation

The insurance platform was built around the assumption of one-time sales, and a tactical decision was made at the platform's conception to not allow policy updates.

Later, on entering the home contents insurance market, policy updates became a concrete requirement.

The request was scoped and the team were about to start work: "Implement a policy update feature so we can move into this new market."

My Intervention

I said no, preventing and deprioritising the feature.

I knew that a different team's backlog included a more complete rejuvenation of the policy service, and insisted that this be a priority use case for that later section of work.

The Compromise

Rather than concretely block the new vertical, I worked with the operations team on a temporary workaround. I understood that moving into a new vertical would have a low sales rate whilst brokers became familiar with the products, giving us time to automate the process and then later update the product.

Iteration one: instead of truly updating the policy, we would cancel the policy manually and create a new one, maintaining the original policy end date. This issues a refund and takes a new payment — two interactions with the customer's bank, which must be explained back to the customer when they request the update, plus a manually sent email containing the updated terms of the contract.

(continued on slide 5)

The Compromise (Continued)

Iteration two: Zapier automations trigger the policy cancellation and the new policy creation, and the platform's email configuration service controls the correspondence — but there are still two transactions.

Iteration three: months later, the full policy service work began — including a more flexible relationship to payments and invoicing, and policies with the possibility of update — at which point the Zapier automations became redundant and could be disabled.

The Outcome

  • Saved several weeks of engineering time and could keep the focus elsewhere
  • During the first stage, fewer than twenty manual policy recreations were required across a month, as predicted
  • The automations then removed the need for manual interaction as demand accelerated, fulfilling hundreds of cases and allowing the same workaround to be applied in other verticals
  • The full platform update then included this functionality natively, rather than creating technical debt in a standalone feature working against the original design of the platform
Demonstrates Constructive pushback — protecting engineering priorities while still unblocking the business through a proportionate, temporary compromise.
Example from Alteos

Data Driven Decision Making

Core Principles

  • Stay curious even beyond the scope of your responsibilities
  • Don't fight with the information you uncover, verify it
  • When new information changes the picture, adapt with it

Situation

Over the course of years, we released hundreds of insurance products, and thousands of partnerships.

Having released a new "partner portal" feature, I was investigating directly in SQL to understand how many partners had signed up, and which products those partners were selling.

I found a very strong trend: 90% of sign-ups were selling one of two core product lines.

Discovery

I dug deeper, and the data revealed something significant.

90% of our sales came from 10% of partners selling from just a couple of product lines.

Our sales team was incentivised to bring partners in, but many of those partnerships would sign up and then become dormant, rather than generating sales.

Intervention

I immediately flagged this to leadership:

  • Strategically, engineering had often created single-use features for partnerships that yielded results in the immediate aftermath, but ultimately fell off and became irrelevant — not all partnerships, but many.
  • I proposed a change to the incentivisation structure of the sales team, prioritising policies created, not partnerships signed.

The Result

  • The company pivoted to a deeper, sustained sales focus
  • Engineering was given room to push back harder on new partnerships
  • Sales moved to a more relationship-management format, with regular educational visits to existing partners
  • The partner portal feature was expanded with dashboards so individual partners could understand not just what they were earning, but how much more opportunity remained on the table
Demonstrates Following curiosity into the data to surface a hidden business risk, then turning that insight into a concrete change to incentives and strategy.

Example from CEEZER

Prototyping with Cursor

The Situation

The company offers a portfolio page for customers to review and understand the credits being proposed.

This feature hadn't been updated in a long time, and the sales team were supplementing it with spreadsheets to explain the content fully.

The company was also moving from one-time purchases towards long-term off-takes — a more subscription-based model — which the sales page couldn't currently support. As such, I planned to update and restructure this page as part of a wider update to the platform's core tooling.

The Approach

First, I reviewed several exemplar sales spreadsheets and talked to the sales team to understand the current process and the data being communicated uniquely through these complex sheets — the objective was to understand what problems were being solved that could instead be served by platform functionality.

Second, I worked with a freelance designer to collaborate on the UX of the new version.

(continued on slide 3)

The Approach (Continued)

I then prototyped the feature myself directly in Cursor, creating a clickable version of the tool populated with a realistic example dataset.

I took this prototype back to the sales team and discussed the workflow, comparing it to the current process. These discussions involved a number of suggestions for improvement, which were implemented same-day.

(continued on slide 4)

The Approach (Continued)

When the internal team were happy with the result, we ran a series of short customer interviews with willing externals from established partnerships, including Siemens.

These interviews focused on two things: how clear the information was as presented in the prototype, and whether the page would be valuable for the interviewee in communicating the portfolio's value to executives for sign-off before purchase.

The Result

With the prototype well validated, we passed it back to the designer for a second round of UI polish, then finally to engineers to build.

No engineer time was wasted on early iterations, and the feature was updated to solve both the LTO (subscription model) requirement, and remove the use of supplemental offline spreadsheets.

Demonstrates Using rapid, low-fidelity prototyping to validate a redesign with both internal stakeholders and real customers before any engineering time was spent.
Personal project

Vibe Coding Game Development

The Situation

Whilst selectively applying to jobs, I wanted to further develop my "vibe coding" toolkit with a project that went deeply beyond the complexity of a feature prototype.

I chose game development as a way to experiment with deep, iterative development in the era of AI — designing a game I'd actually enjoy playing myself (a challenging isometric roguelike survival game).

The focus was on good practices: learning the strengths and pitfalls of vibe coding in greater depth.

The Approach

Tools used: Cursor.

I had a clear vision for what the game should ultimately be, but developed it in slow, iterative layers. First, a player object in a bounded, navigable space. Then a more conveyor-like space that scrolled infinitely. Then terrain obstacles scattered throughout that had to be navigated.

And more, and more — each layer adding complexity to the core gameplay loop, quality-testing at each stage that the loop still felt satisfying to play.

The Pitfall

After a few days of work I had a mostly working, playable version, and was making small balancing tweaks. But each change was aggressively consuming tokens, even when the change itself was trivial. I decided to investigate why.

I found that, architecturally, the game was built around one core "main.js" file, eight thousand lines long — and the system needed to parse the whole file for every tiny change. Hugely inefficient.

The Solution

I decided to do a full refactor: swapping from Cursor to Claude Code, starting from zero, and cannibalising sections of the original code for use as reference material.

Instead of giving the agent full control of the architecture, I set clear constraints on code conventions — splitting functionality into small, granular files that could be easily grepped by the agent when making an update or a balancing change.

The Outcome

Changes are now far faster and far cheaper, which let me get to a stable version.

The game is playable via GitHub Pages here. Fair warning: it's very challenging by design — frequent death and deep replayability are very much the expectation of the genre.

Demonstrates Recognising an architectural bottleneck through cost signals, and re-scoping tooling and conventions mid-project rather than pushing through with a flawed foundation.
Example from CEEZER

Agent Supply Funnel Data Ingestion

The Situation

CEEZER operate in the carbon credit market, managing both supply and demand.

The supply funnel works directly with carbon credit project developers around the world, ranging from startups in Silicon Valley to farmers in Angola.

Pricing and availability details are communicated in a wide range of formats — API, emails with attachments, emails without attachments, even WhatsApp messages — then copied manually into the system by the supply team. This is a time-consuming, error-prone process, and a clear candidate for automation.

The Risk

Data accuracy is deeply important here, as small inaccuracies in a given credit price can add up to discrepancies in value of thousands of dollars over the course of a portfolio.

As such, QA is required in addition to a person in the loop.

The Approach

Tools used: the Claude Agent SDK.

  • Created a dedicated inbox for data from suppliers
  • Built a multi-agent system where two Claude agents attempt the same task in parallel, running twice a day, creating a table of all new inputs
  • Established a linear workflow where these tables are systematically evaluated row by row: where they match, they're input directly into the system; given any discrepancy, they're flagged for human intervention — the operator is shown the original correspondence and asked to pick and/or correct the input before the data is added to the system

The Outcome

From twelve-plus hours a week of operator time spent on data entry, to almost zero — with thousands of inputs successfully ingested into the system with perfect accuracy.

Demonstrates Designing an agent-plus-human verification loop that removes manual toil without sacrificing the accuracy a financial process demands.
